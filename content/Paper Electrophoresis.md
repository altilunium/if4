Paper electrophoresis is one of the simplest techniques used to separate amino acids in a mixture. 

In this method, a small drop of the amino acid solution is carefully placed onto a strip of filter paper and allowed to dry completely.

Next, the paper is moistened with a buffer solution set to a specific pH value, which helps maintain the desired charge state of the amino acids during separation. The ends of the paper strip are then dipped into electrode compartments containing the buffer solution, ensuring good electrical contact.

When a direct electric current is applied across the paper, the amino acids migrate along the paper according to their net electrical charge at that particular pH. Amino acids that carry a net positive charge (cations), such as lysine, arginine, and histidine, will move toward the cathode (the negatively charged electrode). Conversely, amino acids with a net negative charge (anions), like aspartic acid and glutamic acid, will migrate toward the anode (the positively charged electrode).

Once the separation is complete, the paper strip is removed from the apparatus and dried. To visualize the separated amino acids, the paper is sprayed with a reagent called ninhydrin and then gently heated. Ninhydrin reacts with free amino groups to produce colored spots—typically blue or purple—which reveal the positions of the individual amino acids on the paper.

### Procedure

1. Prepare the Electrophoresis Chamber
Begin by filling the compartments of the electrophoresis apparatus with a Tris buffer solution at a concentration of 0.07 M and a pH of 7.6. Tris buffer (short for tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane) is commonly used because it maintains a stable pH, ensuring that the amino acids stay in the desired ionization state during separation.

2. Apply the Amino Acid Solution 
Carefully place 10 to 20 microliters (μl) of the amino acid solution onto the center of the filter paper strip. This small volume (1 microliter = one-millionth of a liter) allows for precise application without causing the spot to spread too widely, which would blur the separation. The solution should have a concentration of about 1.0 mg/ml in Tris buffer, providing enough analyte for clear detection.

3. Moisten the Paper with Buffer
Evenly wet the filter paper with the same Tris buffer solution. This ensures good electrical conductivity along the paper and helps maintain a consistent pH environment throughout the run.

4. Run the Electrophoresis
Connect the apparatus to a direct current (DC) power supply. Perform the electrophoresis for approximately 3 hours at a field strength of 8 volts per centimeter of paper length. For example, if your paper strip is 20 centimeters long, set the voltage to around 160 volts. This voltage gradient provides enough force to drive the migration of amino acids without overheating or damaging the paper.

5. Visualize the Separated Spots
Once the run is complete, remove the paper and gently spray it with ninhydrin reagent. Ninhydrin reacts with free amino groups in the amino acids to form colored complexes. Allow the paper to air dry overnight in a well-ventilated area, or accelerate color development by heating it at 100 °C for 5 to 10 minutes. The separated amino acids will appear as distinct blue or purple spots, each corresponding to a different amino acid in the mixture. Examine the Rf value for each separated spot on the paper. Rf stands for Retention factor (also known as the Ratio of fronts). It is a dimensionless value used in chromatography to express how far a compound travels on the paper compared to how far the solvent moves. The solvent front is simply the distance from the original spotting point to the furthest point reached by the solvent or buffer during the run. 

The Rf is calculated using the formula :

Rf = Distance traveled by the solute (spot) / Distance traveled by the solvent front
​
To make the numbers easier to read and compare, Rf values are often multiplied by 100. For example, an Rf of 0.18 would be reported as 18.

In the context of paper chromatography, a common mobile phase used for separating amino acids is a mixture of n-butanol, acetic acid, and water in a ratio of 4:1:1. In this mixture, n-butanol acts as the main organic solvent that helps dissolve the amino acids, acetic acid adjusts the pH to influence the ionization state of the amino acids, and water helps control the overall polarity of the system. This specific balance allows the different amino acids to separate effectively based on their varying solubilities and interactions with the stationary phase.
