As early as 1968, when natural gas reserves began to decline, the US Bureau of Mines began to examine the issue of how to extract unconventional gas resources. Several major studies commissioned by the Federal Power Commission, the Energy Research and Development Administration, and the US Department of Energy in the 1970s suggested that the resource base of unconventional natural gas could be very large and that efforts to develop unconventional resources should be encouraged and subsidized.

In October 1973, the Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries (OAPEC), led by Saudi Arabia along with other Arab oil producers, imposed an oil embargo against the U.S. and other countries that supported Israel during the Yom Kippur War. The oil embargo stimulated the federal government to begin to adopt a series of policies to deal with the energy crisis. 

The seed of the shale gas boom was planted in the late 1970s when the US government decided to fund R&D programs and provide tax credits for developing unconventional natural gas in response to the severe natural gas shortage at the time. 

It was goverment policies that stimulated the development of shale gas in the Appalachian and Michigan Basins and helped develop some key technologies, such as microseismic fracture mapping.

However, it was the private entrepreneurship from Mitchell Energy that played the primary role in developing the Barnett play in Texas, and it was the successful development of the Barnett play that jump-started the shale gas boom.

### Fort Worth Basin, Texas

George Mitchell entered the oil and gas industry through the encouragement and tutelage of his older brother Johnny. After graduating from Texas A&M University in 1940, earning a degree in petroleum engineering with an emphasis in geology, Mitchell served in the Army Corps of Engineers during World War II. He began working with his brother on oil prospects while still a member of the Army Corps. He and Johnny established their first company, "Oilr Drilling Inc." in the late 1940s, with George generating drilling prospects and his brother finding investors.

Ellison Miles -- an acquaintance of George's from his time at A&M, started a drilling company in north Texas after leaving the Army. He and business associate, John A. Jackson, became aware of the potential for a large stratigraphic gas accumulation in Pennsylvanian-age Atoka conglomerates in the Fort Worth basin of Texas and communicated their thoughts to George.

> "Stratigraphic gas accumulation" means that natural gas is trapped in the rock layer due changes in the rock's characteristics, not because of obvious structural traps like folds or faults. A stratigraphic trap happens when variations in rock type create a seal that traps hydrocarbons. For example, a porous sandstone might pinch out into an impermeable shale, trapping gas in the sandstone. "Pennsylvanian-age" refers to a geologic time period within the Carboniferous, about 323 to 299 million years ago. Its name comes from the U.S. state of Pennsylvania, because rocks from this age are very well exposed and were first studied there, especially the widespread coal-bearing rock layer. "Atoka" is a name for a rock formation in the Fort Worth Basin, primarily consisting of sandstones, conglomerates and shales. "Conglomerates" are coarse-grained sedimentary rocks made up of rounded gravel-sized clasts cemented together. They often have good porosity and permeability, making them potential reservoirs for hydrocarbons.

Oil Drilling Inc. put together the necessary money and took their first lease in the Forth Worth basin, a 3,000-acre tract in southwest Wise County.

In 1952, George Mitchell's journey to the Barnett began with the drilling of the first well on this lease, the D.J. Hughes No 1. The well encountered multiple members of productive conglomerates. Within 90 days of this discovery, Oil Drilling Inc. acquired 300,000 acres in the north Texas area. Subsequent test confirmed the significance of the find.

### Natural Gas Pipeline

Oil Drilling Inc. was faced with several obstacles at this point, the most significant of which was a market for the gas. Since the early days of drilling in the area, Lone Star Gas Company had dominated gas gathering and marketing in north Texas, transporting it to the Dallas area. Operators had little choice but to sell their gas at Lone Star's terms, which included seasonal takes. Mitchell understood that to make this new play commercial, he had to have year-round sales under a guaranteed contract. 

Mitchell immediately set out to break Lone Star's stranglehold on the north Texas gas market. In 1955, he was successful in negotiating a very favorable contract with Natural Gas Pipeline (NGPL) for their Chicago market. Terms of the agreement provided a take-or-pay provision (they buyer must either take a specified minimum quantity or pay for that amount anyway, even if they don't take it), loans to begin development drilling in the play, a term of 20 years from commencement of sales from a dedicated area, and a price of 13 cents/Mcf (1000 cubic feet) of gas, which was substantially higher than the existing local market. Oil Drilling Inc. would be responsible for delivering pipeline-quality gas to a gathering system to be built by NGPL and tied into their trunk line in Oklahoma. This major line moved gas from the Hugoton/Panhandle fields of Kansas, Oklahoma, and Texas to the Chicago market, where gas was used during the winter months and injected into strorage fields during the rest of the year.

The pipeline-quality gas provision forced the company to get involved in the gas processing business. At that time, most of the gas in the area contained a lot of heavier hydrocarbons, giving it a high heating value (~1,300 BTU per cubic foot). To sell it on the pipeline network, they had to remove ("strip") the natural gas liquid (NGLs) to reduce the BTU to the required 1,000-1,100 range, as if the gas is too "hot" (too high BTU), it can damage equipment or not burn consistently for end-users.

Prior to the first sale of gas through the contract on 17 December 1957, Oil Drilling Inc. changed its name to Christie, Mitchell & Mitchell to reflect H. Merlyn Christie's involvement in the company. Over the next 35 years, the company experienced a number of name changes, but maintained a continuous drilling program in the Atoka conglomerates.

In 1972, the company name changed again to Mitchell Energy & Development Corp. (MEDC) and was listed on the American Stock Exchange. 

### Fracture Stimulations

Over the years, Mitchell applied fracture stimulations to most of the north Texas conglomerate wells to improve rates, ultimate recoveries, and well economics. In 1979, the company pumped the largest massive hydraulic fracture stimulation on record at the time in an attempt to improve the commerciality of its North Personville field in Limestone County, Texas. The treatment was a gelled water fracture consisting of 1 million gallon of fluid and 2.8 million lb of sand. It resulted in a twofold increase in the well's production rate. This stimulation opened up a new niche, which was unconventional, tight-gas reservoir (low-permeability standstones or carbonates). 

### Contract Obligations

In 1981, Mitchell drilled the discovery well for the Newark East Barnett shale (NEBS) gas field, the C.W. Slay No 1. Originally planned as a Caddo limestone and Atoka conglomerate test, the well was deepened at Mitchell's insistence to evaluate an even deeper formation, called the Viola limestone (Ordovician-age), as an extension to an existing field in the area. When the Viola proved tight (low permeability), Mitchel requested a test of the overlying Mississippian-age Barnet shale, above the Viola. The initial test after acidizing (injecting acid to dissolve rock and open up flow channels to try to improve permeability) was insignificant and the well was shut-in for evaluation.

The C.W. Slay No 1 well was stimulated in the lower Barnett in September 1981 with a 250-ft theoretical fracture half-length (TFHL) treatment. The well tested gas at ~250 Mcf/D, an insufficient rate to justify a connection by NGPL. Wells not accepted by the gas gatherer either had to be shut-in or connected using company funds. Mitchell paid the cost and the well was connected to sales in June 1982. 

The Barnett's initial production coincided with negotiations between the company and NGPL to improve the gathering system in the contract area to drop line pressure. During the previous 25 years, many of the company's wells reached advanced stages of pressure depletion and were unable to produce against line pressures without the aid of company-installed single-well compression. These individual compressors greatly increased manpower requirements and operations cost.

NGPL indicated a willingness to make the system upgrades if the company could show a minimum of 5 years of drilling potential to meet the contract obligations. Mitchell requested an in-house study of drilling prospects within the contract area to obtain an estimate of the remaining life of the existing conglomerate play. The results indicated that there were sufficient prospects for the 5-year period and probably for an additional 5 years beyond that.

By 1992, however, the company would have a difficult time finding and drilling enough commercial wells to replace yearly production and that would lead to difficulty meeting contractual volume requirements. Mitchell then issued his challenge : "We have 10 years to find a replacement for the north Texas revenue stream." Mitchell was assured that everyone involved understood the critical nature of the situation.

### The Barnett Shale

At that point, no one in the company knew what the replacement would be, but due to the recent test in the C.W. Slay well, the Barnett shale was one of the likely candidates. Over the next decade, the list of candidates grew to include 10 possibilities. The general feeling was that the replacement would not be a single objective, but rather a multitude of objectives.

The effort began with assessing available sources of information. Some of the shallow prospects on the early list could be evaluated using existing well data as new development wells were drilled. That was not the case for the Barnett shale and some of the deeper prospects, since most of the deeper wells in the basin were old and had inadequate well logs. In fact, to save money, even some of the newer wells that used modern logging programs had the porosity tool turned off while crossing the Barnett shale. The absence of good gamma ray and porosity logs in wells significantly hampered regional mapping and evaluation.

When management was informed about the lack of a good database, it was decided that standalone exploratory wells were not feasible. On the other hand, exploratory deepenings of selected development wells could be used as long as development reserves would carry the total well cost. 

During the same time period, members of the team were reviewing publications on the north Texas area as well as the published result from the Gas Research Institute / US Department of Energy (GRI/DOE) Eastern Gas Shale Project. One of the main takeaways from the GRI/DOE projet was that gas shales needed to have open natural fracture network for gas storage and permeability.

The team then initiated a detailed structural analysis for the deep section within the company contract area. Shallow wells were utilized to map the base of the Atoka to see if it might highlight areas where faults, flexure zones and four-way structures could be identified, since those were all regimes where natural fracturing would be expected. Seismic data was obtained, wells were deepened, cores were taken, and a Barnett database began to be built on the shale's organic richness, thermal maturity, structural complexity, source-rock thickness, and petrophysical parameters.

In areas with tectonic activity, the Barnett Shale naturally developed lots of fractures — which could have been good for production because natural fractures can let gas flow more easily. But over geological time, white calcite (a mineral) precipitated inside these fractures. This filled up the open spaces → so the fractures became cemented shut → no flow pathways. So, the natural fracturing didn’t help because the fractures healed themselves with mineral deposits. When fractures fill with calcite, extra mineralization can spread into the surrounding rock pores. This reduces the tiny spaces (pores) where gas could be stored. So the healing process makes the whole rock less effective as a reservoir. As a result, in these “healed” structural areas, it takes more pressure to fracture the rock than in undisturbed zones. Core samples confirmed that the rock’s porosity is very low and mostly microscopic.

In the evaluation of the 30 test wells, the company's geology team was able to identify gas-mature areas, and the engineering team experimented with varying types and sizes of stimulations. Initially, foam fractures were pumped. But the need for longer fracture wings caused the TFHL to exceed the capacity of the fluid to carry the large volume of sand used. The fractures were growing longer than the foam could support, so they weren’t getting enough sand all the way out to hold the fractures open. Therefore, engineering was forced to go with nitrogen-assisted gel fractures, which is thicker and can suspend larger volumes of sand for longer distances.

By the end of 1986, the company had pumped fracture stimulations with TFHL designs as long as 1,500 ft. In many of these wells, as the fracture lengths were increased, there was a commensurate increase in initial rates. The 1,500-ft design frequently yielded initial gas rates of 900 Mcf/D to 1,100 Mcf/D, so the company decided to standardize on this size and monitor production and develop decline curve and economic models.

### Slickwater Frac

Mitchell engineers started to question the gelled water frac procedure after an experiment in 1996. In 1997, Mitchell engineers began to experiment a new approach to fracturing. The new frac method was a slickwater frac developed by Union Pacific Railroad Corporation (UPR) for use in the low-permeability Cotton Valley sandstone formation of East Texas. This new frac approach used a large amount of water and a small amount of sand as the proppant. This procedure allowed UPR to achieve at least the same amount of gas production as before with a substantially reduced capital expenditure. It was a radical concept at the time because the general consensus among engineers at the time was as much proppand (sand) as economically possible had to be placed in order to maximize fluid conductivity to the wellbore. Gel can carry plenty of proppant, but water can carry very little. Yet the lower viscocity fluid in slickwater could leak into tiny natural cracks, generating a more complex web of fractures. 

### Horizontal Wells

Mitchell had joined with GRI/DOE in 1991 to drill a horizontal test well in the Barnett to evaluate the commercial application of the technique in a shale well. The well proved to be non-commercial after a single induced gel fracture in the uncemented portion of the cased lateral.

However, after several years of monitoring the well, the team believed that horizontals might be utilized in areas with poor or absent bottom fracture containment and recommended drilling two horizontals, one perpendicular to induced-fracture direction, and one parallel. The wells were drilled in 1998 in a noncommercial Barnett area. Both were initially stimulated with volumes commensurate to a vertical well. The completions indicated the perpendicular lateral to be superior to the parallel well and were deemed to be mechanical successes, in that the stimulation stayed in zone, but commercial failures. 

Horizontal wells were not always successful for a number of reasons, the most important of which is the lack of understanding of the geological setting into which the lateral is being drilled. 3-D seismic data are needed to understand the geological setting.

Unfortunately, additional horizontal testing was put on hold while the company evaluated strategic alternatives for improving stockholder value, by investigating the possibility of a sale or merger. 

### Shale Revolution

In August 2001, a merger with Devon Energy was announced.

The Mitchell-Devon merger greatly accelerated the development of the Barnett play. The merger sent a strong signal to the industry that the Barnett play had great potential, but what turned the potential into a real boom was a widespread application of horizontal drilling, microseismic frac mapping, and 3-D seismic imaging, in addition to the slick water frac used extensively by Mitchell Energy.

Barnett was the first large-scale shale gas play. Its success triggered the shale revolution. Companies rushed to apply these techniques to other shale plays. This added tens of trilions of cubic feet of gas and billions of barrels of oil to U.S. reserves. Thanks to the shale boom, the U.S. became the world's largest natural gas producer by 2009. By the late 2010s, the U.S. also became the largest oil producer. The country became a net exporter of natural gas and crude oil products for the first time in decades.

Over a 17-year period, Mitchell successfully proved the Barnett shale source rock to be a viable and commercial play and then moved to enhance its value through the application of new and existing technology, ramping up production, and acquiring significant additional acreage positions in prospective areas. The Barnet shale resource play not only had a profound effect on the US' energy supply at a critical time, but also resulted in a complete reevaluation of the world's hydrocarbon energy supplies.

George Mitchell’s achievement in the Barnett Shale was remarkable because it changed the way people thought about shale gas forever. In the early 1980s, geologists knew that shale was the source rock for oil and gas, but they didn’t believe it could be a good reservoir rock — it was just too tight for hydrocarbons to flow freely. Mitchell’s team didn’t accept that. They set out to build a new, practical knowledge base that went far beyond the old “source-rock” data. They studied the rock in detail — its mineralogy, porosity, natural fractures, how gas moved through the matrix — and they learned what actually made production possible in a shale.

One of Mitchell’s key insights was that you didn’t have to rely on open, natural fractures to make shale gas economic. Up to that point, most exploration models for tight rocks depended on finding sweet spots where natural fractures were already open and connected. But Mitchell showed that you could induce your own fractures through hydraulic fracturing — even in areas where the natural fractures were cemented shut or absent. This completely changed what areas could be drilled.

Another important lesson was understanding the role of fracture barriers — layers of rock above or below the target zone that help keep hydraulic fractures contained. If fractures grow out of the shale into surrounding formations, you waste energy and materials on rock that won’t produce hydrocarbons, and you can even connect to unwanted water zones. By recognizing how fracture barriers worked, Mitchell’s team could design completions that stayed in the productive layer, improving well performance and economics.

Finally, Mitchell’s team didn’t just rely on what existed — they combined new ideas and old technologies in ways no one had done before. They refined horizontal drilling, tested different types of fracturing fluids, and pushed the use of slickwater fracs — a method that was against the conventional wisdom at the time — to reduce costs and improve recovery. Together, these innovations made shale production not just possible but profitable, proving that tight rock could become a massive source of domestic gas.

In short, Mitchell’s achievement wasn’t about one breakthrough but about piecing together the geology, the engineering, and the practical know-how to turn a once-unlikely rock into a major, reliable reservoir. This became the playbook for the entire shale revolution that followed.

He has been referred to as "The Father of Fracturing". However, that is not accurate. Fracturing was originally proposed and utilized by others in the 1940s, expanded upon by many more over time, and used by thousands before George Mitchell applied the process to the first Barnett shale well. 

If George Mitchell to receive a title for his achievements, it should be "The Father of the Shale Gas Revolution."
