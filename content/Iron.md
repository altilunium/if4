### Ore

Very small amounts of iron occur on earth as pure "native" metal (meteoric iron, telluric iron). Instead, basically all iron was smelted from iron ores, which required considerable processing to produce a pure metal. 

The most commonly used iron ore was hematite, with goethite and limonite close behind. Rarer, but still used was magnetite and siderite. Many of these ores can be spotted visually. While they can all yield iron, some of them yield that iron easier than others. 

All of these can occur in big rock deposits, but may also occur as "bog iron" where oxidation occurs in acidic environments, such as swamps and bogs. Bog iron is formed when ground-water picks up iron from iron-ore deposits, where that iron is then oxidized under acidic conditions to form chunks of iron minerals (geothite, magnetite, hematite, etc), typically in smallish chunks. Bog iron is much easier to smelt because it contains fewer impurities than iron ore in rock deposits, but the quantity of iron available from bog iron is relatively low. 

Finding ore in the pre-modern period was generally a matter of visual prospecting, looking for ore outcrops or looking for bits of ore in stream-beds where stream could then be followed back to the primary mineral vein.

Lacking an understanding of geology, for instance, meant that pre-modern miners, if the ore vein hit a fault line, had to resort to sinking shafts and exploratory mining in an effort to find it again. In many cases, ancient miners seem to have simply abandoned the works when they couldn't manage to find it again.

### Mining

Once the ore was located, mining tended to follow the ore. For ore deposits in veins, that typically means digging shafts (vertical tunnel), galleries (horizontal passage), or trenches -- if the deposit was shallow -- that follow the often irregular, curving patterns of the veins themselves. 

All of this digging had to be done by hand. Iron-age mining tools (picks, chisels, hammers) fairly strongly resemble their modern counterparts and work the same way. 

For rock that was too tough, the typical expedient was "fire-setting", which remained a standard technique for removing tough rocks until the introduction of explosives in the modern period. It involves constructing a fuel-pile up against the exposed rock and then letting it burn, typically overnight. The heat splinters, cracks and softens the rock. 

While Bronze Age mines tend to stay above the water-table, iron-age mines often run much deeper, which raises all sorts of exciting engineering problems in ventilation and drainage. Deep mines could be drained using simple bucket-lines, or more sophisticated methods, from the Roman use of screw-pumps and water wheels to Chinese use of chain-pumps. Ventilation shafts were often dug, with the use of either cloth fans or lit fires at the exits to force circulation.

### Smelting

The charcoal -- a nearly pure mass of carbon -- takes some heat and some oxygen from the air and produces carbon dioxide and a bunch more heat, which both heats up the iron ore and the rest of the charcoal. This process is rapidly going to be oxygen starved, and so some of these reactions are going to produce carbon monoxide, which reduces the ore (Fe2O3 + 3CO -> 2Fe + 3CO2).

Most of the impurities from the ore are going to be silica and alumina. In modern furnaces, a flux (typically limestone) is introduced into the mixture because it will readily bind to these compounds to get them out of the iron.
